Direction D comes out of this, not out of taste. These are structural conventions — how the page is organised and what it does — rather than anyone's visual style. D keeps B's voice entirely and rebuilds the spine underneath it, around two jobs: explain the cider, then say where it is.

Angry Orchard — behind a consent wall
Austin Eastciders gates its whole site behind an age check I did not answer on your behalf, so I saw only that screen. Angry Orchard sat behind a cookie wall for the same reason. Shacksbury refused the connection. Everything below comes from the four I got inside.
01

Everyone gates on age. You don't.

Three of three that I reached put up an age check before the site: Downeast asks for a full date of birth, Artifact asks “Are you 21 or older?” with yes and no, Austin Eastciders blocks the entire page until you answer. This is not decoration — it is what the category treats as table stakes.

In D: a designed first screen in the brand voice, not a bolted-on modal. It is the first thing anyone sees, so it should look like you.
02

An announcement bar sits above the logo

Downeast runs the current release name across the top of every page. Eden runs a shipping offer with an expiry date. It is one line of text above the masthead, changed weekly by someone in marketing, and it is where the urgency lives.

In D: B's gold ticker moves above the masthead and becomes the always-current slot. It also answers finding 5 — the homepage stops going stale.
03

“Where to buy” is a top-level nav item

Downeast calls it Where to Buy. Citizen calls it Cider Locator. Both put it in the main navigation with four or five siblings, not in the footer. Downeast and Eden also carry search, account and cart icons up there — but their business is partly direct-to-consumer and yours is overwhelmingly distribution.

In D: the locator takes the space a cart would have taken — a filled button in the masthead, a link on every cider, and the whole bottom of the homepage. This is finding 1 treated as a design problem instead of a routing bug.
04

Tasting notes, written like wine

Eden gives every cider a structured note — “notes of candied lemon rind, apricot jam, peach marmalade, clove” — and a second paragraph on how to drink it. Your data has a single flavor string per cider: “Juicy & Semi-Sweet”. That is a shelf-talker, not a tasting note.

In D: a proper note on every tile, plus ABV and a named sweetness stop. This needs writing — it is the one change here that costs copy rather than code.
05

A release year turns a product into an event

Eden names products by vintage — Heirloom Blend 2024, Northern Spy Barrel-Aged 2023 — and marks one “currently available to cider club only”. A date and a run size tell you everything without a price attached. You already have exactly this product in the Rare Apple Series and describe it as “sells out fast”, but the site gives it no date, no year and no way to know whether this season's run still exists.

In D: a release calendar with dates, vintages, run sizes and an honest “gone for the year” state. Pure information — and the single most useful thing you could add, given you are already halfway to it.
06

The can supplies the colour, not the interface

Citizen sets each can on its own solid panel — blue, maroon, mustard — drawn from that label. The page stays neutral and the products do the shouting. It is a better answer to finding 6 than mine was: rather than neutralising the mint and yellow badges, give each cider a ground colour that comes from its own artwork.

In D: six colour-blocked tiles, each ground sampled from that can. Your label art is your best asset and it is currently floating on grey.
07

The taproom is a destination, not a footer address

Downeast gives its taproom a full section with hours and location. Citizen names its pub — The Press House — and puts it in the main nav. You have a taproom photograph sitting in the repository and no section anywhere that sends anyone to it.

In D: a taproom strip with hours. The hours shown are placeholders — I don't know your real ones.
Two patterns I left alone

The direct-to-consumer shop. Eden sells bottles from the page — prices, quantity steppers, add to cart, a shipping-offer bar. It works because ice cider is a $27 gift bought online. Your business is cans on shelves across six states, so D deliberately leaves the cart out and spends that attention on the locator instead. The club is the one place a checkout still belongs.

Editorial minimalism. Artifact goes the opposite way entirely — monospace body type, a serif display, a dictionary definition as the centrepiece, enormous whitespace, and no commerce at all beyond an email address. It is confident and it suits them. It is also roughly Direction A, and it is the wrong answer for a brand with twelve SKUs, a club and a taproom to sell. Worth knowing it exists as a pole.

Conventions only. Nothing in Direction D reproduces any of these brands' layouts, illustration, colour or wording — it applies what the category has settled on to your own assets and to Direction B's voice.
